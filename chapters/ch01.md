### Chapter 1: The Day Transparency Became Law

**Extract from OEP Specification v1.0:**  
_All internet-facing computational services must run exclusively within compliant secure hardware enclaves. Source code must remain universally open and verifiable under public audit protocols. Any deviation constitutes a direct violation, subject to severe sanctions as detailed in GECA Article 14.2._

---

Around the globe, headlines celebrated Transparency Day as a victory for digital trust. News feeds overflowed with jubilant proclamations: "The End of Digital Deception," "A New Era of Trust," "Open Code, Open Future." Social platforms buzzed with virtual champagne toasts and techno-optimistic predictions. For most of the world, July 1, 2035 marked a revolution.

In a move widely seen as a direct response to the growing outrage from 2020s data scandals—like the infamous TikTok hearings and Facebook whistleblower revelations—governments and regulators rushed to champion the Open Enclave Protocol. Skeptics warned of hidden costs, but few listened as politicians and tech giants alike promised never again would personal data be weaponized against its users.

Jess Carver watched the livestream from her modest studio apartment in Brooklyn, a familiar heaviness settling in her chest. The event broadcasting from the marbled halls of the United Nations in Geneva showed a coalition of world leaders shaking hands beneath banners emblazoned with a glowing OEP logo, all smiles and photo-ready postures. The global ratification of the Open Enclave Protocol was complete.

On her secondary display, business headlines streamed past: "Google and Meta Complete Final Migration to Public Code Repositories," "Amazon Web Services Now Runs 100% on Verified Enclaves," "Apple's Walled Garden Finally Truly Transparent." It had been fifteen years of heated debate since the Cambridge Analytica scandal of the early 2020s first sparked serious calls for algorithmic transparency. What had begun as fringe activism following repeated data breaches at Twitter and Facebook had culminated in this moment, as tech giants that once fiercely guarded their proprietary algorithms now publicly displayed their source code for all to see.

Her chat interface flickered with comments:

> **@EnclaveWatch:** Finally, no more secret algorithms controlling what we see!

> **@DataSovereign:** Anyone else wonder why China isn't at the table?

> **@CodeBreaker420:** Wake up, sheep. Hardware enclaves mean they'll just hide the spyware deeper.

Jess sighed and muted the stream. Her wristband buzzed softly—another notification:

> **OEP Alert:** Your banking service, CitizenTrust, has transitioned to mandatory enclave compliance. Review their code audit [here].

The device on her wrist—the sleek Tesla Neurallink V2 that had replaced clunky smartwatches—projected a holographic prompt showing a compliance certification. Three years ago, everyone had marveled at the first generation of these devices; now they were ubiquitous, with even the basic models requiring migration to OEP-compliant firmware.

Jess knew the signs all too well—the rapid heartbeat, the dull knot in her stomach. It had been the same seven years ago, back when she'd first uncovered the algorithmic mess lurking behind Pathway Health's "confidential" diagnostic platform.

Seven years ago, Jess had been younger, more naïve—fresh out of MIT and eager to prove herself. Pathway's marketing had promised flawless AI-driven diagnoses, safeguarding patient data with what they'd called "secure computing vaults." But Jess, analyzing a leaked snippet of source code from a disgruntled insider, had discovered the truth: behind reassuring language lurked biased algorithms silently weighting patient insurance profiles and subtly prioritizing profitable treatments over medically necessary ones.

She vividly remembered scrolling through the exploit—a carefully hidden bypass embedded deep in the system's supposedly secure hardware layer. Pathway had relied on public trust in their opaque tech, protected by layers of complexity and NDAs. Jess's anonymous report had ignited a firestorm, prompting congressional hearings and ultimately bankrupting Pathway, though the executives landed softly elsewhere.

Pathway had grown out of the primitive healthcare algorithms of the early 2020s—distant descendants of Apple Health, Fitbit, and early telemedicine platforms that had exploded during the COVID-19 pandemic. By the late 2020s, every major hospital system had adopted AI diagnostic tools with promises of democratizing healthcare access. Only after scandals involving racial bias in the Mayo Clinic's diagnostic tools and UnitedHealth's discriminatory recommendation engines did anyone begin seriously questioning these systems. But by then, healthcare AI had become too embedded to easily remove.

Jess had followed the gradual deterioration of healthcare privacy throughout the 2020s, watching as HIPAA exemptions were carved out first for AI "research purposes," then for "personalized care optimization," and finally for the behemoths like Pathway that claimed their proprietary diagnostic systems were too complex for regulatory oversight. The patterns had been obvious to those paying attention, but most people had been too dazzled by the promise of 24/7 healthcare access to question what happened behind the algorithmic curtain.

The Open Enclave Protocol was supposed to be different—open-source, fully transparent. But Jess couldn't shake the feeling that they were repeating the same mistakes, just with better marketing.

Jess rubbed her temples, a familiar knot of anxiety tightening in her chest. This blind optimism felt painfully familiar—the same bright promises, the same glossy assurances. Just like when Pathway Health had sworn their algorithms were fair and secure, right before those same algorithms nearly denied her brother Alex life-saving treatment based on hidden profit calculations. She still remembered his pale face in the hospital bed, how close she'd come to losing him because of code she couldn't see. 

The day after her exposé broke, she'd visited him in the hospital to find news vans camped outside. Camera flashes had illuminated her path through the hospital doors as reporters shouted questions: "Ms. Carver, do you regret ruining a successful healthcare company?" "Was it worth destroying thousands of jobs?" She'd felt both triumphant and hollow, torn between pride in exposing the truth and guilt about the chaos she'd unleashed.

Alex himself had mixed feelings. "You're on every news channel," he'd said weakly when she entered his room, gesturing at the muted television where her face appeared repeatedly in news segments. His attempted smile hadn't reached his eyes. "My little sister, the corporate killer." Though he'd tried to sound proud, she could sense his discomfort with the sudden spotlight—the unwanted attention intruding on his already difficult recovery. Later, he'd admitted his anxiety: "Some of my doctors won't look me in the eye anymore, Jess. Like I'm the reason their colleagues at Pathway lost their jobs." The personal costs of public vindication had lingered long after the headlines faded.

And Alex was one of the lucky ones. After her exposé went public, she'd met dozens of families who weren't so fortunate—elderly parents bankrupted by suddenly denied coverage, desperate siblings launching crowdfunding campaigns for treatments the algorithm deemed "suboptimal ROI cases," children who didn't survive the appeals process. For these families, transparency wasn't some abstract technical ideal—it was literally life or death. Now everyone was celebrating transparency, but Jess knew better than most how good technology was at hiding its true nature.

From her apartment window, Jess could see into Marissa's Corner Café across the street, where a crowd had gathered around the wall-mounted display. The café owner was gesturing at her tablet, explaining to regulars about mandatory audits for her point-of-sale system. "Third update this morning," Marissa muttered, "each one more expensive than the last."

The conversations overlapped in a chorus of confusion and concern:

"My Venmo's been down all morning. PayPal's still processing my rent from three hours ago."

"Remember the CryptoBank collapse? Six million locked out for weeks. They promised this wouldn't happen again."

"The daycare payment is due today. If it doesn't go through..."

Near the window, two college students hunched over a phone. "Look, I ran the code audit on Instagram," one said, scrolling through dense text. "This is supposedly how it picks what to show me, but I can't understand any of it."

A professor at the next table overheard. "That's the point, isn't it? Transparent in a way that's opaque to anyone who's not a developer."

Outside the café, a delivery driver apologized to a customer—orders stuck in limbo while the app underwent "compliance updates." The driver shrugged helplessly. "Six orders frozen. Could be minutes, could be hours."

Jess turned away from the window, a bitter smile playing at her lips. The gap between the grand promises of Transparency Day and these everyday confusions told its own story. For most people, the OEP meant little more than system updates and temporary inconveniences.

The chaos reminded her of Pathway's aftermath—how quickly celebration had turned to confusion. She'd become a reluctant public figure overnight. Some thanked her with tears; others blamed her for the disruption. "Heroes don't get death threats," she'd told one journalist who'd asked how it felt. The public wanted simple narratives—brave whistleblower defeats corporate villain. Reality was messier. Just like today's compliance chaos revealed how poorly equipped most people were to navigate sudden shifts in systems they depended on but didn't understand.

She tapped into her bank's newly-public GitHub repository. Thousands of lines of code unfurled before her, technically visible yet functionally impenetrable to anyone without specialized knowledge. Jess scrolled through the sanitized functions, feeling both vindicated and uneasy. In theory, she now knew exactly how her money was handled—every transaction traceable within the enclave. But her years of security work had taught her that true vulnerabilities rarely announced themselves; they lurked in the spaces between perfectly legitimate lines of code, camouflaged by complexity.

As she scanned the repository, her mind drifted back to the cascade of tech scandals that had gradually eroded public trust over the past decade. Cambridge Analytica had been just the beginning—a preview of the systemic problems to come. She remembered the Google TargetID scandal of 2026, when whistleblowers revealed the company had secretly bypassed its own privacy protections to track users across supposedly anonymized services. The revelation that personal health queries were being sold to insurance companies had triggered class-action lawsuits and congressional investigations that dominated headlines for months.

Then came the Meta Emotional Response Patent fiasco in 2028, when documents leaked showing the company had been using facial recognition and typing patterns to build "emotional vulnerability profiles" for targeted advertising without disclosure. The timing couldn't have been worse—coming just days after their CEO had personally testified that such capabilities "absolutely did not exist" in their systems.

The Amazon Prime Security Breach of 2029 had been the final straw for many—400 million customers' home security camera footage, voice assistant recordings, and purchase histories exposed in what white-hat hackers called "the most predictable breach in history." When security researchers demonstrated how easily AWS "secure" infrastructure could be compromised using basic exploits, public rage had reached a boiling point.

"Trust us, we've fixed it" had been the tech giants' refrain for years. Each scandal followed the same cycle: revelation, outrage, congressional hearings, nominal fines that amounted to rounding errors on quarterly earnings, promises to "do better," and then—inevitably—another, often worse breach a few months later. By the early 2030s, surveys showed public trust in major tech platforms had plummeted to single digits.

When the OEP was first proposed, Jess had been cautiously hopeful. Unlike previous half-measures, it had seemed to address the core issue: the opacity that allowed abuses to flourish. But sitting here on Transparency Day, watching the celebration and chaos unfold simultaneously, she couldn't shake the sense that they'd merely entered a new phase of the same old pattern.

The code was visible, yes. But who was actually reading it? Who had the expertise to spot the subtle tricks that might be hidden within millions of lines? And most importantly—what about the hardware itself, the black boxes where the code actually ran?

The sound of hurried footsteps in the hallway broke her concentration—Liam's familiar erratic rhythm. Ever since the OEP announcements had ramped up, his anxiety had been escalating. The footsteps halted. Then came the knock—urgent, almost frantic.

Her musings were interrupted by a frantic knock at the door. Opening it revealed Liam, her neighbor from across the hall—a former software engineer who'd burned out after exposing a hidden spyware scheme at a major social media platform two years earlier. Usually cheerful and irreverent, his face was taut, eyes rimmed red with exhaustion. He shifted his weight nervously from foot to foot, fingers drumming an anxious rhythm against his thigh. A fine sheen of sweat glistened on his forehead despite the building's air conditioning. They'd known each other from activist circles for years, but he'd only moved into the building six months ago, drawn by both the reasonable rent and the comfort of having an ally nearby.

"Did you see this?" He thrust his tablet at her with trembling hands, his voice cracking slightly. His eyes darted past her to scan her apartment, as if expecting hidden surveillance devices in the corners. The headline on his screen blinked ominously:

> **"Rogue TikTok Clone Discovered Running Illicit Dark Enclave in Montenegro."**

Since leaving the corporate world, Liam had quietly freelanced for activist organizations, auditing leaked code and tracing funding channels. He'd expected attempts to subvert Transparency Day—but not this fast.

Liam's gaze flickered nervously to the window. His shoulders hunched forward as if carrying an invisible weight. "After what happened at my last company—" he began, then stopped abruptly, swallowing hard. His fingers curled into a tight fist. "I was on the team that built those systems, Jess. I wrote some of that code myself, believing we were protecting user privacy." His voice dropped to a hollow whisper. "Then I discovered the spyware that quietly harvested user data for months. Data from children, activists, journalists—people who trusted us. I helped violate that trust without even knowing it."

He ran a shaking hand through his disheveled hair. "I still get emails sometimes—from people whose lives were ruined when that data leaked. Activists imprisoned in Belarus, journalists who had to flee their homes, a teacher in Turkey who lost everything when her private messages were published. Real people destroyed by code I helped write." His voice broke. "I can't watch another promise crumble into betrayal. People's trust can't keep getting exploited like this."

Liam shook his head bitterly, recalling his days at the social media giant. "After the endless congressional hearings over how our app used AI to manipulate engagement—remember the whole 'Facebook Files' from 2021?—I thought transparency was the solution. But they're just burying the secrets deeper."

Jess raised an eyebrow. "Already? That was fast."

"This isn't just some black-market app," Liam whispered urgently, stepping inside and shutting the door. "Look who funded it."

She scanned down, pulse quickening. Her hands grew cold, stomach tightening into a familiar knot of dread. It was the same visceral reaction she'd had years ago when uncovering the Pathway scandal. Buried deep in the article was a troubling detail: the rogue enclave had funding links to major Western tech companies, suggesting they were intentionally dodging transparency laws.

"This has ByteDance fingerprints all over it," Liam murmured, referring to TikTok's Chinese parent company. "Remember the encryption wars of the 2020s? When Signal and WhatsApp fought against government backdoors while China was building their own isolated digital ecosystem?" He paced anxiously. "This is the same playbook, just evolved. Beijing never accepted the Western transparency mandates. They've been developing hardware workarounds since the first OEP drafts leaked."

Jess tapped the screen, enlarging a section of the article. "They're positioning it as a 'digital sovereignty' issue—just like Russia did with their walled internet in the 2020s." She scrolled further. "But it's not just geopolitical. Look at the corporate investors—all the same companies that fought end-to-end encryption regulations for years, claiming privacy concerns while harvesting user data through backdoors."

Jess stared at the screen, her breath catching. The words blurred as her mind raced ahead, connecting invisible dots. This wasn't opportunistic sabotage—this was coordinated, methodical. Someone with resources had prepared for Transparency Day by building a parallel system designed to operate in the shadows.

She'd anticipated resistance, even expected corporations to drag their feet on compliance, but this level of organization suggested something far more dangerous. The companies named weren't obscure startups but established players with everything to lose if caught violating GECA regulations. What could be valuable enough to risk such penalties?

Jess looked up slowly, meeting Liam's anxious gaze. A chill spread through her chest as the implications crystallized. "Transparency Day isn't even twelve hours old."

"You know what this means, right?" Liam asked, nervously rubbing the back of his neck. "It's like 2025 all over again—when those secure messaging apps claimed they were fully encrypted while secretly building government access points."

She nodded grimly. "They're building a parallel network—a black enclave web. Same concept as the Dark Web, but with industrial backing and nation-state resources."

"Exactly," Liam said. "And if they're caught, it could fracture everything before it's even begun. The Chinese digital sphere already operates under different rules. If Western companies are building bridges to those dark enclaves, we're not just looking at corporate malfeasance—we're witnessing the balkanization of the global internet, despite all the unification promises."

"Remember how fast Pathway faded from headlines?" Jess said, her voice hardening. "Everyone was furious, demanded change, and two months later it was business as usual. The execs who denied life-saving care all landed at new startups with bigger salaries. How long before people forget this too?"

Jess felt an old, familiar fear rising—the same sensation she'd experienced during the Pathway investigation when she realized how deep the deception went. But this was different. That had been one company's algorithm; this was potentially the foundation of the entire digital ecosystem. They weren't exposing isolated breaches anymore; they were standing at the edge of a digital warzone, with billions of users caught in the crossfire.

Jess stared at the screen, the implications spiraling out before her. Today was supposed to mark the beginning of trust rebuilt, of a transparent, accountable internet.

"Welcome to the new reality," she said quietly, her voice tinged with bitter amusement.

Liam slumped onto the small couch, shaking his head. "And it's barely noon."

"Yeah," Jess muttered, eyes drifting back to the flashing headline. "Long day ahead."

She looked up at Liam, noticing how his usually casual posture had stiffened, tension pulling at his features. "If they've already got hidden enclaves running," she began cautiously, "it's because they found a vulnerability in the protocol itself."

She glanced back at the muted livestream, where champagne glasses clinked and hands still shook in congratulation. The celebration continued in its hermetically sealed bubble, divorced from the digital tremors already beginning below the surface.

"Alright," Jess said, straightening decisively. "Looks like we've got work to do."

"What's our first move?" Liam asked.

"We dig deeper," she replied, already reaching for her laptop. Her voice steadied despite the tremor in her hands. She thought briefly of Alex—now working as a patient advocate after his recovery, using his experience to help others navigate the healthcare system. The ordeal had changed him, left him with chronic fatigue that flared unpredictably, but he'd found purpose in preventing others from suffering what he'd endured. Never again. She couldn't let more people suffer what her family had endured. "Let's find out exactly how far this goes—and who's really pulling the strings."

**London, UK - 6:00 PM GMT**

Across the Atlantic, in a sleek glass office overlooking the Thames, Marcus Linley watched the same UN livestream with growing unease. As head of compliance oversight for the UK's Digital Transparency Office, he'd spent two years preparing for today—yet something felt wrong.

-----BEGIN PERSONAL LOG-----

Diary Entry – Marcus Linley – June 30, 2035

Tomorrow, Transparency Day arrives—supposedly my life's greatest achievement. Yet, sleep evades me. Sophie believes this is a new beginning, but doubts gnaw at my conscience. Did we move too fast? Did we miss something crucial? Tomorrow, billions trust us. God, let us not betray them.

The cost of failure isn't just professional disgrace. With every family dinner, every proud glance from my daughter, the personal stakes grow heavier. The world calls me the "Architect of Digital Trust," yet tonight I'm consumed by questions no one dares ask publicly: What if the hardware itself betrays us? What if our transparency creates the perfect disguise for something worse?

I've staked everything on this. Not just my career, but the very notion that we can rebuild a trustworthy digital world from the ashes of decades of exploitation. If we've failed, I'm not sure there's a third chance.

-----END PERSONAL LOG-----

His decade-long journey from young MP grilling Facebook executives during Cambridge Analytica to becoming the architect of global digital trust had taught him one thing: every promise of security hid new vulnerabilities. The 2031 AI Financial Crash that triggered three days of market shutdown had finally forced the world to act. Now his daughter Sophie called him a hero. Marcus wasn't so sure.

Marcus Linley stared at the notification blinking insistently on his screen, the crimson alert pulsing like an accusation:

> **[Urgent] Compliance Anomaly Detected: Possible Hardware Exploit Identified in Initial Enclave Audits**

His pulse quickened, a cold sweat breaking across his forehead as he read the words again. His mouth went dry. Years of carefully crafted assurances, meticulous audits, countless speeches about "unhackable systems"—was he about to become complicit in the greatest breach of public trust in digital history? He removed his titanium-framed glasses with trembling fingers and pinched the bridge of his nose, feeling the onset of a tension headache that matched the gravity of the situation.

His eyes drifted to the framed photo on his desk—his daughter Sophie in her university graduation gown, beaming with pride, his wife Laura at her side. Just last night at dinner, Sophie had eagerly described her new job at a medical tech startup that relied entirely on enclave certification. "Dad, it's amazing—patients finally trust the system because of your work," she'd said, raising her glass in a toast. "To the man who made the digital world trustworthy again." The memory now twisted like a knife in his chest. His family's faith in him had always been absolute. If the system collapsed, Sophie's career would be among the first casualties—along with the patients who had finally begun to believe in the promise of secure healthcare data. This wasn't just a professional crisis; it was intensely personal.

The London skyline glittered through the office window, reflecting the city's deceptive tranquility. Marcus turned back to the report flagged in crimson on his tablet screen, his reflection in the glass showing a face suddenly aged by worry. It detailed anomalies detected by auditors at one of the major cloud providers during preliminary enclave validation:

> **Audit Alert Summary**  
> Initial checks suggest someone secretly changed the enclave hardware without documentation. Further investigation is mandatory.

"Perfect," Marcus murmured sarcastically, his voice barely audible even to himself. He stood up, legs stiff with tension, and began pacing slowly toward the expansive window. His fingers worked unconsciously at his wedding band—a nervous habit that emerged only in moments of extreme stress. Below, London bustled as usual, oblivious to the seismic digital shift already underway. Just weeks earlier, Marcus himself had assured the Prime Minister—and by extension, the British public—that enclave technology represented a revolutionary leap forward in trust.

Now, he wasn't so sure.

His secure line buzzed gently, interrupting his thoughts. With a wary glance at the encrypted caller ID, Marcus tapped the screen, seeing the encrypted, anonymized caller ID he had been expecting.

"Tell me you've got good news," he said sharply, unable to keep the strain from his voice.

A digitally masked voice replied, calm but measured, "Depends how you define good. We traced the compromised enclaves to hardware manufactured under license in Taiwan. On the surface, the chips look legitimate, but someone embedded a hidden backdoor deeper inside the hardware."

Marcus removed his glasses entirely, setting them carefully on the desk as he pressed his fingertips against his closed eyelids. The dull throb behind his eyes intensified into a stabbing pain. Every word from his anonymous source felt like another nail in the coffin of his career—of the entire global initiative he'd championed. "State-sponsored?" he asked, his throat so tight the words barely escaped.

"Too early to confirm," the voice replied. "But yes, someone extremely sophisticated is playing a long game. Whoever it is, they understand enclave tech intimately."

Marcus thought instantly of the geopolitical chess match underpinning the entire OEP initiative. The TSMC Super Fab in Taiwan had been the focal point of international tension for over a decade, with the 2027 Taiwan Strait crisis nearly escalating into open conflict when Chinese military exercises had blocked semiconductor shipments. The resulting global economic shock had forced unprecedented cooperation—Western powers had pushed through the International Semiconductor Security Pact, creating a neutral oversight committee with representatives from both China and the U.S. to ensure Taiwan's chip production remained untainted by any single nation's influence.

Those security protocols were supposed to be foolproof, with multiple layers of international verification for every chip designed for enclave computing. Intel, AMD, and Taiwan's manufacturing giants had retooled their entire production lines at staggering cost to meet the new transparency standards. Every step of the manufacturing process was supposedly monitored, recorded, and independently verified by the global oversight committee.

Marcus remembered the contentious debates around the 2024 chip shortages and the subsequent geopolitical brinkmanship over semiconductor supply chains. The OEP had been designed specifically to prevent another round of global mistrust, yet here they were, teetering dangerously close to repeating history.

And yet, here they were. If someone had managed to compromise the chips despite all those safeguards, the implications went far beyond technical concerns. This was potentially an act of digital warfare.

"Are we talking industrial espionage, national-level sabotage, or black-market profiteering?" Marcus asked, his fist clenching unconsciously as he paced faster now, heart pounding in his chest. His carefully cultivated public persona—calm, authoritative, reassuring—was crumbling in the privacy of his office as each scenario emerged more alarming than the last.

"Still unclear. Could be all three," the voice said coldly. "But there's chatter indicating that this isn't isolated. Other enclaves—already compliant, audited, and certified—could be affected."

Marcus stopped pacing. A chill settled over him as he stared at his faint reflection in the window. The implications were staggering. If the very hardware at the heart of the protocol was compromised, the trust so painstakingly built into every line of code was already a sham.

"Are regulators aware yet?" Marcus asked, keeping his voice steady despite the tension.

"Not officially. Only select individuals within compliance teams know. But leaks are inevitable. You have maybe a week, two at most, before the story breaks wide open."

Marcus nodded slowly. "Then we have to move quickly. Pull the compromised units, quietly. I'll handle damage control from here."

The line disconnected abruptly, leaving Marcus alone with a profound sense of dread. He knew the utopian vision behind the Open Enclave Protocol wasn't just at stake—it might already be shattered. He turned away from the window, toward the quietly humming computer bank that filled half the office. His entire life's work had aimed at precisely this—trust through transparency. Now, a hidden adversary was turning that vision on its head.

Marcus reopened the file, pausing briefly before typing a confidential message: "Immediately halt approval for all enclave hardware made at the Taiwanese plant until we thoroughly investigate."

He clicked send and exhaled deeply. The war had begun sooner than anyone anticipated.

**Washington DC, USA - 1:00 PM EST**

In a climate-controlled data center outside the capital, systems engineer Elaine Chen frowned at her monitor. The verification logs for a batch of new enclave processors showed an anomaly—a fractional latency spike during attestation that didn't match the benchmarks. These weren't just any processors—they were the latest generation Intel Secure Haven chips, direct descendants of the SGX technology that had dominated confidential computing in the 2020s before AMD's Epyc Fortress architecture had briefly taken market leadership.

Elaine expanded the diagnostic panel, scrolling through pages of technical data. The processors had come from the TSMC Super Fab outside Taipei—the same facility Marcus Linley had just flagged for emergency suspension. She remembered the supply chain crisis of 2027, when Chinese military exercises in the Taiwan Strait had halted semiconductor shipments for three months. That crisis had directly led to the international oversight protocols now governing Taiwan's chip manufacturing—the same protocols that were supposed to make hardware exploits impossible.

-----BEGIN LEAKED DOCUMENT-----

Internal Memo (CONFIDENTIAL) – TSMC Super Fab Operations Team
Date: June 28, 2035 (3 days before Transparency Day)
Subject: Urgent—Production Yield and Verification Issues

Attention Team Leads:

Recent batches of Intel Secure Haven chips exhibit higher-than-acceptable variance during cryptographic attestation tests. She flagged the anomaly in the system, then hesitated before categorizing it. After a moment's consideration, she marked it "non-critical" and added a note: "Probable calibration artifact from mass deployment. Monitor but proceed." Elaine glanced at the dozens of other alerts flooding her queue on Transparency Day. No time for edge cases today. She swiped to the next ticket, the anomaly already forgotten.

Little did she know, her decision would become one of many small oversights that would cascade into something far larger—a crack in the foundation of trust that the entire world was celebrating.

A businessman in a tailored suit jabbed repeatedly at his wristband, which displayed a warning about service disruptions due to "OEP compliance upgrades." A university student looked up from his device, catching the eye of the elderly man sitting opposite. "Did you see this?" he asked, leaning forward to show his screen. "They're suggesting the hardware itself might be compromised."
